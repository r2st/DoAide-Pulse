"Free marketing" is a misnomer that costs small teams more than any ad budget. Nothing in this article is free. It is all paid for in the scarcest thing a small SaaS has, which is the founder's attention, and the reason most content strategies fail is that they were priced as free and then abandoned when the bill arrived.

So the useful question is not "what content marketing can I do for no money". It is "what content compounds enough to justify the hours, given that I will only sustain a few hours a week".

That constraint eliminates most of the standard advice immediately.

## What the constraint eliminates

**Publishing on a content calendar you invented.** Three posts a week is a schedule for a team with a content hire. A solo founder committing to it produces four good posts and then stops, and the stopping is worse than never starting, because a dead blog is a trust signal in the wrong direction.

**Writing for keywords you have no authority on.** A new domain targeting a broad, commercially valuable term is competing against sites with a decade of accumulated authority. You will not win "project management software" and the effort spent losing is unrecoverable.

**Chasing platforms that were never yours.** Reach on somebody else's feed is rented, priced by an algorithm you do not control, and revoked without notice. Rented reach is worth having, but building on it as the foundation is how a channel disappears overnight.

**Generating volume.** Producing more mediocre posts is cheaper than it has ever been, which is precisely why it no longer works. When everyone can generate a competent 1,200-word overview of a topic, a competent 1,200-word overview of a topic is worth approximately nothing.

## What compounds

Three kinds of content reliably repay the hours for a technical product.

**The thing you learned building it.** You solved a problem in a domain most people do not have to think about. The specifics — the constraint that turned out to matter, the approach that failed, the number that surprised you — cannot be generated by anyone who did not do the work. This is your only genuinely defensible content, and it is sitting in your commit history.

**The decision framework for a purchase you are part of.** Not "why we are best". The actual structure of the choice: the categories of tool, the questions that separate them, and an honest statement of where your product is the wrong answer. That last part is what makes the rest credible, and it is the part everyone omits.

**The reference page nobody has written well.** Every niche has a handful of concepts that are explained badly everywhere. A clear, correct, unhurried explanation of one of them earns links for years, because people cite what saved them an afternoon.

All three share a property: they are downstream of work you already did. That is the only thing that makes the economics survive a small team.

## The distribution move that matters most

Write once, publish to your own domain, syndicate everywhere else with a canonical URL pointing home.

The mechanics are worth being precise about, because getting this wrong is common. When the same article exists on your blog and on a developer platform with no canonical link, the two copies compete for the same query, and the platform — with its enormous domain authority — usually wins. You have handed your best asset to somebody else's SEO. Setting `rel=canonical` on the syndicated copy tells search engines which one is the original, so the platform's traffic becomes referral traffic to you instead of a competitor for your own keyword.

That single configuration is the difference between syndication being a growth channel and being a slow transfer of your equity to a platform.

The corollary: publish to your own domain *first*, or at least make it the canonical target. A piece whose canonical points at a platform is a piece you are renting.

## Where technical buyers actually are

Narrower than the advice suggests. For developer-facing products, the honest list is: developer publishing platforms with real organic reach, one or two communities where your specific users already congregate, and whatever aggregator serves your niche.

The rule for communities is uncomfortable and non-negotiable: participate for months before you post about your product, or do not post about your product. Communities are extremely good at detecting people who arrived to extract from them, and a burned community does not un-burn.

Social distribution is worth automating and worth low expectations. A link post to a small following is a modest referral bump, not a channel. Its value is compounding an audience slowly, which only works if you are still doing it in a year.

## Measuring without analytics budget

Three numbers, tracked monthly.

**Organic sessions to the article.** Slow to move — expect nothing for a quarter — and the only signal that the compounding is real.

**Referral sessions by source.** Tells you which syndication destination is worth the extra step.

**Signups attributable to content.** Crude attribution is fine. The purpose is to distinguish "this article gets read" from "this article gets used", and those are frequently different articles.

What not to track: impressions, and anything on a social platform's dashboard that goes up whether or not the business does.

## The cadence that survives

One substantial piece a month, syndicated properly, indefinitely. That is twelve assets a year that keep working, versus a burst of thirty that stopped in March.

If a month feels slow, it is worth remembering what the alternative actually was — which for most small teams is not a faster cadence but zero, because everything downstream of "the code is live" happens by hand and therefore does not happen.
